\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Many Apache Spark pipelines are \emph{recurring}: the same job runs every hour or every day on
new data. Spark offers many ways to make such a job faster. Two common ones are changing the
number of shuffle partitions and compacting many small input files into a few large ones. A
large body of work automates these steps, either by searching Spark's configuration space
\cite{nguyen2018tuning,xin2022locat,lyu2024optimizer,herodotou2020survey} or by deciding how
tables are laid out and maintained \cite{gruenheid2025autocomp,meduri2026pto}.

But optimization has a price. Finding a better partition count means running the job under
several settings, and compaction reads and rewrites the data. A faster job is therefore not
automatically a cheaper one: the one-time cost of the action must be paid back by the savings
of later runs. A job that runs once is rarely worth tuning, while a job that runs a thousand
times almost always is. In between, teams often keep Spark's defaults or apply an
optimization by habit, without comparing the costs.

Earlier work covers parts of this question. Spark tuners try to keep their own overhead small
\cite{xin2022locat,li2025loftune}, and recent lakehouse systems weigh the benefit of
compaction or layout changes against their cost \cite{gruenheid2025autocomp,meduri2026pto}.
Fekry \emph{et al.}~\cite{fekry2020tune} showed that tuning cost must be spread over future
runs. What is missing is a controlled study that applies one break-even rule to two
\emph{different kinds} of action, one that changes how Spark executes and one that changes how
data is stored, and that shows when each one pays off.

We address this gap with CAO-DF (Cost-Aware Optimization Decision Framework). Given a
workload's features and the expected number of future runs $N$, CAO-DF picks one of three
options: keep the default (\emph{static}) configuration, tune the number of shuffle partitions
(action A1), or compact the input files (action A2). It compares the total cost
$\Copt + N \cdot \Crun$ of each option and reports the optimization break-even point (OBEP),
the smallest $N$ at which an action pays for itself. We do not claim that Spark tuning or
compaction is new; our contribution is a careful measurement of \emph{when} these two
existing actions pay off. We ask three research questions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{RQ1:} How do data volume, file fragmentation, shuffle volume and run frequency
        affect the cost and benefit of partition tuning and file compaction?
  \item \textbf{RQ2:} Can a workload-aware cost rule lower total cost compared with a static
        Spark configuration?
  \item \textbf{RQ3:} Under what conditions does each action become worth its cost?
\end{itemize}
We test two hypotheses. \textbf{H1:} the cost and benefit of partition tuning and of
compaction vary with workload features. \textbf{H2:} a workload-aware rule that skips actions
whose savings do not cover their one-time cost lowers total cost compared with the static
configuration on unseen workloads.

\noindent Our contributions are: (1) a simple, explainable decision rule for two Spark actions
of different kinds, with an explicit break-even test and a noise floor; (2) measurements from
250 runs, in randomized blocks, of how volume, fragmentation and shuffle volume change the
value of each action; (3) a break-even analysis under two cost measures and three price
levels; and (4) a test of the rule on four unseen workloads (120 more runs), with all code and
data released.

In short, the default of 200 partitions was the slowest setting on every workload; a full
search for a better value paid for itself within three runs; compaction paid for itself at
the first run on fragmented input and never on single-file input; and CAO-DF beat or tied the
static configuration in 14 of 16 unseen cases under P1 and in all 16 under P2. Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work,
Sections~\ref{sec:problem}--\ref{sec:framework} define the cost model and the rule,
Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the experiments, Section~\ref{sec:results} gives the
results, and Sections~\ref{sec:discussion}--\ref{sec:conclusion} discuss them, list the
limitations and conclude.
