\section{Experimental Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Partition tuning (A1)}
Fig.~\ref{fig:a1} shows the run time of each partition setting. The default of 200 partitions
was the \emph{slowest} setting on all four workloads. The best setting was 7.9--11.3$\times$
faster: 4 partitions on both large workloads (7.95$\times$ and 8.47$\times$), 1 partition on
small\_high (11.31$\times$) and 2 on small\_low (10.66$\times$). The optimum is a wide plateau:
settings from 1 to 8 were all much faster than 200. Even 400 partitions was 2.8--3.7$\times$
faster than 200. The amount of data shuffled was the same for every setting and nothing was
spilled to disk, so the differences come from the number of shuffle tasks and files, not from
the data. Measured by executor time (P2) instead of wall-clock time, 1 partition was best on
every workload. Five of the 32 cells were noisy (CV $> 0.2$), each because of a single slow run
at 1 or 2 partitions; removing that run left the ranking of settings unchanged (Spearman
$\rho \ge 0.976$).

\subsection{File compaction (A2)}
Fig.~\ref{fig:a2} compares runs before and after compaction. On fragmented input (10 or 50
files) compaction cut the median run time by 66.2\,\% and 66.4\,\% for small data and by
24.6\,\% and 32.3\,\% for large data, and it was faster in all 5 paired blocks. On single-file
input it changed nothing ($-1.7\,\%$ and $-0.3\,\%$). The benefit grows with the number of
files for large data and is already saturated at 10 files for small data. The compaction job
itself took only 0.14--1.29\,s. Executor time fell from 61--62\,s to 9\,s (large) and from
31--32\,s to 4\,s (small). More input files create more scan tasks, and at 200 shuffle
partitions every map task writes 200 shuffle files, so the A2 benefit is partly an
\emph{interaction} with the default partition setting. Six of the 18 cells were noisy.

\subsection{Break-even points}
Table~\ref{tab:breakeven} combines the savings and one-time costs. A full partition search
costs 2.1--2.5 static runs under P1 (1.8--2.2 under P2), but it saves 87--91\,\% of every
later run, so it pays for itself after three runs ($\Nstar = 3$; 2--3 under P2). At $N = 50$ it
lowers total cost by 82.5--87.0\,\% (P1) and 91.9--95.1\,\% (P2). Compaction costs only
6--20\,\% of a run under P1 and less than 2\,\% under P2, so on fragmented input it pays for
itself at the first run. On single-file input it never pays off. The one finite value there
($\Nstar = 11$, large, P2) comes from a 0.9\,\% ``saving'' that is within the noise and is
removed by the noise floor. Fig.~\ref{fig:tc} shows three typical total-cost lines, and
Fig.~\ref{fig:map} places every workload on one break-even map. Halving the price or raising
it by half changed no $\Nstar$, as expected from (\ref{eq:obep}).

\subsection{Effect of the cost measure}
Fig.~\ref{fig:proxy} compares the two cost measures. For tuning they agree closely. For
compaction, P2 shows much larger savings (85--88\,\% against 23--67\,\%), because compaction
removes many short tasks (counted by P2) but the machine is still reserved while the job runs
(counted by P1). The two measures give the same $\Nstar$ for 11 of 14 workload--action pairs,
and the same act-or-not decision for all of them once the noise floor is applied.

\subsection{Validation on unseen workloads}
Table~\ref{tab:validation} and Fig.~\ref{fig:validation} compare CAO-DF with the baselines on
the four unseen workloads at $N \in \{1, 5, 10, 50\}$ (16 cases per cost measure). CAO-DF was
cheaper than or equal to the static configuration in 14 of 16 cases under P1 and in all 16
under P2, and its average reduction was within 2.4 points (P1) and 0.4 points (P2) of the
hindsight oracle. Over all cases together, total cost fell by 74.1\,\% (P1) and 87.9\,\% (P2).
Performance-only always chose tuning; at $N = 1$ this cost 2.4--2.7$\times$ the static cost
under P1, because the search costs more than one run saves. CAO-DF avoided all four of these
losses. Its choices changed with $N$ as the model predicts: nothing or cheap compaction for few
runs, and tuning for many runs.

CAO-DF lost to static only twice, both under P1 at $N = 1$, when it chose compaction for the
500K/25-file and 2M/5-file workloads (regret 14.5\,\% and 23.9\,\%). On these two workloads
compaction made the run 1.7\,\% slower and only 3.3\,\% faster, while the nearest study
workloads suggested savings of 34\,\% and 23\,\%. Estimated tuning savings were within 3.4 points of the measured ones on
every workload, and the best partition count was again small (2 or 4). In a leave-one-out
test on the study workloads, the rule agreed with the hindsight choice in 80\,\% of cases
(64/80); all misses were on the two single-file A2 workloads, whose nearest neighbour has 10
files. Setting $\delta$ to 0, 5\,\% or 20\,\% gave identical choices.

\subsection{Hypotheses}
\textbf{H1} is \emph{supported for compaction} on wall time: its benefit ranged from none on
single-file input to 66\,\% on fragmented input, and it grew with the number of files for
large data (going from 10 to 50 files added 7.7 points, 95\,\% CI 4.4--17.8) but saturated at 10 files for
small data. On executor time the small-data trend was not monotonic, so there it is only
partly supported. For tuning, H1 is \emph{supported by our pre-registered test}: the best
setting (1--2 partitions for small data, 4 for large) and the speed-up differed between
workloads (small-high 11.31$\times$, CI 10.41--13.42, against large-high 7.95$\times$, CI
7.26--8.75). However, every workload gained by a large factor, so the \emph{decision} to tune
did not depend on the workload. \textbf{H2} is \emph{supported}: on unseen workloads CAO-DF
was no worse than static in a clear majority of cases, and its mean reduction was 47.3\,\%
(CI 30.5--62.3) under P1 and 72.2\,\% (CI 59.6--81.8) under P2.
