\textbf{A1.} Searching the eight candidate settings costs 2.1--2.5 static executions under P1
(1.8--2.2 under P2), while the chosen setting saves 87--99\,\% of every subsequent execution.
The search therefore pays for itself after two or three executions on every workload, and
after a single execution if the setting is known without a search. At $N = 50$ the searched
setting lowers the estimated total cost by 82.5--87.0\,\% under P1 and 91.9--95.1\,\% under P2.

\textbf{A2.} Compaction costs between 6\,\% and 20\,\% of one execution on fragmented data
under P1, and less than 2\,\% under P2, so it pays for itself at the first execution whenever
the input is fragmented. For single-file inputs it never pays off. The only finite break-even
point among them, $\Nstar = 11$ for the large single-file workload under P2, comes from a
saving of 0.9\,\% that lies inside the run-to-run variation, and is an artefact a decision rule
must not act on (Section~\ref{sec:framework}).

\textbf{Price and proxy.} Scaling the price by 0.5 or 1.5 leaves every $\Nstar$ and every
decision unchanged, as expected when $\Copt$ and $\Crun$ use the same proxy; only the absolute
savings scale. The two proxies give the same $\Nstar$ for 11 of the 14 workload--action pairs
and, once savings within noise are discarded, agree on whether to act for every workload at
every $N \in \{1, 5, 10, 50\}$. They differ in two respects. First, P2 always selects a single shuffle partition for A1,
whereas P1 selects 1--4, because extra partitions shorten wall-clock time but add task
overhead. Second, P2 attributes a much larger relative saving to compaction (85--88\,\% versus
23--67\,\%): fragmented input multiplies the task-seconds spent by the executors, which P2
charges in full, while much of that work runs in parallel and so adds less to wall-clock time.
